Weekend Wanderings: 1873 Echoes? No

September 7, 2026

I just finished reading Liaquat Ahamed's 1873.

Coincidentally, Satya Nadella name-checked the book on Microsoft's FY26 Q4 earnings call, calling it "the book to read."

Ostensibly Nadella sees some overlap with the AI boom.

And right off the bat, in the intro, Ahamed writes: "the $500 million that went into U.S. railway bonds annually during the boom years of the early 1870s would today be the equivalent of $600 billion, roughly what is projected to be invested by major tech companies in 2026."

All due respect to the Microsoft boss and the book's author, I'm not buying the comparison.

First, Microsoft is funding its capex out of free cash flow.

Second, by Ahamed's telling, Jay Cooke — the American financier who helped hock Treasury debt during the Civil War and was later tapped to finance the Northern Pacific buildout — had one main innovation: selling railroad bonds to retail, using 1,500 salespeople and 1,300 subsidized newspapers, because he couldn't raise institutional capital. The debt was widely dispersed but shunned by big investors.

Third, railroads were long-lived assets that took decades to generate revenues. Lots of capital upfront, profits far in the future. AI is also a big upfront cost, but it's already generating revenues at a rapid rate.

Fourth, chasing down Ahamed's footnotes — one annoying thing about the Kindle text is that there aren't any footnotes, just a references section at the end — turns out the U.S. railroad buildout was driven by federal subsidies.

And not at the margin. Federal loans ran $16,000 to $48,000 for every mile of track laid. They nominally carried 6 percent interest, but an anomaly in how the legislation was drafted made a substantial portion effectively interest-free, with no repayment due for thirty years. On top of that came public land grants totaling 175 million acres — about 7 percent of the land area of the contiguous forty-eight states, worth some $200 million at prevailing prices and far more later.

Here's the part that matters for the analogy: those land grants served as collateral for mortgage bonds, dramatically reducing the cost of capital to the railroads. The subsidy wasn't a side dish. It's what made the bond boom possible in the first place.

The bottom line is that lots of people keep throwing around the railroad analogy as sophisticated analysis of the AI buildout. I don't think the story works.

For me, 1873 is a more interesting tale about bold bankers (the Rothschilds), international capital markets, and the pitfalls of the gold/bimetallic standard era — not a cautionary tale about capex-led growth.

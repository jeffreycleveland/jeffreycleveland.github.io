1873: The Crash Wasn't the Catastrophe

August 31, 2026

I've been reading Liaquat Ahamed's 1873, and one number in the front matter stopped me cold.

To convert figures from that era into 2026 magnitudes, Ahamed suggests multiplying by roughly 1,200. Which means the $500 million a year that poured into U.S. railway bonds during the boom of the early 1870s is about $600 billion today — roughly what the major tech companies are projected to invest in 2026.

That is a remarkable coincidence of scale. It is also, I think, the wrong place to stop reading.

Because the most useful thing about 1873 is not that a capital-spending boom ended badly. Capital-spending booms often end badly. It is that the crash itself was survivable, and the policy response was not.

The setup will sound familiar

The quarter century from 1848 to 1873 was the first genuine era of globalization. The four dominant economies — Britain, the United States, France, and Germany — grew 80 percent collectively. World trade expanded fivefold.

Savings rates climbed everywhere: Britain from 8.5 to 13.5 percent of GDP, Germany from 9 to 14, the United States from around 15 to above 20. All that saving pushed down the cost of capital. Real long-term interest rates in London fell by half, from the 6 percent that had prevailed before the 1840s to about 3 percent through the 1850s and '60s.

Cheap capital did what cheap capital does. Global investment in railroads and other infrastructure rose from about $1 billion a year in the early 1850s to nearly $4 billion by the late 1860s. Total invested capital across every form — shares, government bonds, bank loans, trade credit, mortgages — went from under $20 billion to over $50 billion.

Here is the detail I keep coming back to: this was overwhelmingly a credit story, not an equity story. By the early 1870s, shares listed on the London, Paris, and New York exchanges were worth about $10 billion. The bond market was worth roughly $20 billion, and it had grown sixfold in twenty years. Railroads accounted for something like $10 billion of the $20 billion in new debt issued during those two decades.

A generation of middle-class savers, still scarred by the British railway mania of 1845 and by Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions, wanted steady income and no drama. They got bonds. The system obliged by manufacturing an enormous quantity of them.

The accelerant

What turned a long expansion into a genuine bubble was a fiscal shock. After the Franco-Prussian War, France paid Germany an indemnity of roughly $1 billion — into a $4 billion economy, over about two years, and equal to one and a half times the total German currency in circulation.

Half of it, some $500 million, went out to individual German states to retire their debts, dumping cash directly into private hands. Banking regulations were liberalized at the same moment, letting institutions hold less specie against note issue.

The results were about what you'd expect:

Between 1871 and 1873, roughly 850 new companies were incorporated in Germany — five times the number formed in the entire preceding century. About 450 of them, worth nearly $1 billion, listed in Berlin. By the end of 1872, planned construction in Berlin would have housed a city of nine million people. Berlin's actual population had not yet reached one million.

Vienna was worse. Prime real estate had been doubling every eighteen months since the late 1860s. Land in the city center went for as much as fifteen times its pre-boom price. In February 1873, the Tagblatt ran the arithmetic and concluded that if all the capital raised for construction were actually deployed, Vienna would need to triple in size.

Ahamed's line on this is the best short description of a capex bubble I've read: markets overshoot because individual investors fail to account for the hundreds of others reacting the same way. The high returns attracted so much capital into construction that it almost guaranteed no one could make any money.

Then it broke

Vienna went first, on Friday, May 9, 1873. Bank stocks fell 45 percent in a day.

New York followed in September, when Jay Cooke & Co. — the largest investment bank in the country, fiscal agent to the U.S. government — failed. Cooke insisted to the end that he faced a liquidity problem, not a solvency problem. He may even have been right. It didn't matter; nobody would lend. The exchange closed for ten days. With no central bank, a nationwide banking collapse was averted only because the New York Clearing House improvised a form of emergency currency for settlement among its members.

Cornelius Vanderbilt, interviewed at his home on Park Avenue that evening, blamed the "great many worthless railroads started in this country." Building railroads from nowhere to nowhere at public expense, he said, is not a legitimate undertaking.

The visible damage was in stocks. The real damage was in bonds. Within five years, more than half of the $2.2 billion of railway bonds listed on the NYSE would default, costing investors close to $600 million.

And yet the recession was mild

This is the part that surprised me most.

Of the four major economies, only the United States had a meaningful downturn, and the peak-to-trough decline in industrial production was barely 6 percent. Railroads kept adding a thousand miles a year. Coal production actually rose. Stocks recovered half their losses in early 1874.

By the standards of the nineteenth century, 1873 was shaping up to be an ordinary panic — one more entry in a long series of setbacks that left little permanent mark.

The blunder

What made 1873 the beginning of a twenty-year depression rather than a bad year was a monetary decision, taken almost absent-mindedly, in the middle of the wreckage.

The pre-1873 system was bimetallic. Money rested on both gold and silver, which meant global monetary conditions were not hostage to the supply of any single metal. France, holding around $1.4 billion in gold and silver — a fifth of the world's monetary reserves — sat at the center as the swing country and stabilizer.

Within a couple of years of the crisis, every country in continental Europe abandoned silver, and the United States suspended its monetary use at almost exactly the same time. What began as a bilateral rivalry cascaded into a wholesale reordering of the global currency system.

The effect was an enormous, unintended squeeze on global liquidity. Over the following six years, prices fell 20 to 25 percent worldwide. In the United States, wholesale prices fell 35 percent. One economic historian called it "the most drastic deflation in the memory of man — in the long history of money and prices from the Middle Ages to the present, there is nothing like it." Milton Friedman attributed nearly all of it to that single decision.

Nor did it stop. Consumer prices in Britain and the United States, already down more than 15 percent by the early 1880s, fell another 15 percent over the following decade and a half. Peak to trough, prices ended up a full third lower.

The timing could hardly have been worse. Policymakers dropped silver just as silver was becoming plentiful and gold scarce, depriving the system of liquidity precisely when it needed it.

What deflation actually does

It is worth being careful here, because the effects were not uniformly bad, and that is exactly why the politics turned so ugly.

Falling prices were good for wage earners in cities, whose nominal wages fell but whose purchasing power rose. They were very good for thrifty middle-class savers holding government bonds.

They were terrible for anyone who owed money. The ubiquitous fall in prices produced a giant redistribution of wealth from debtors to creditors — and watching the bankers most responsible for the crisis profit most handsomely from its aftermath is what turned ordinary distress into populist fury.

Growth, oddly, was fine. The four largest economies grew at roughly the same average rate over the last two decades of the century as they had during the mid-Victorian boom. But each of them spent about a third of that period in recession, and returns to capital collapsed.

U.S. corporate profits fell by more than a third in the five years after 1873 and did not regain their 1872 peak until the 1890s. Real returns to British equity investors went from 13 percent a year in the quarter century before 1873 to under 5 percent in the quarter century after. In the United States, from 11 percent to under 7 percent. The stretch from 1880 to 1896 remains the longest bear market in American history.

Steady growth, persistent recessions, and two decades of poor returns to capital is an unusual combination. It is also a reminder that "the economy is growing" and "capital is being rewarded" are different claims.

The politics arrived on schedule

Republicans lost the House in 1874 in the second-largest swing in that chamber's history. They held the presidency in 1876 by a single electoral vote, via a backroom deal that withdrew federal troops from the South, ended Reconstruction early, and led directly to Jim Crow. The three presidential elections that followed — 1880, 1884, 1888 — were each decided by popular margins under 1 percent, with the party in power losing four of the next five.

Trade policy went the same way. Germany raised grain tariffs from under 6 percent to above 30. French grain levies approached 40 percent. Manufacturing duties, cut from 28 percent in 1850 to 8 percent by the early 1870s, went back up to 15–20 percent. The United States, never a convert to free trade, went to 50 percent with the McKinley Tariff of 1890. Britain held the line on free trade and watched the value of English agricultural land fall by half.

In central Europe, hundreds of thousands of ruined novice investors went looking for someone to blame, and a wave of antisemitism swept the continent — Wilhelm Marr's pamphlet in Germany in 1879, Drumont's La France juive in France in 1886, which sold 100,000 copies in its first year and eventually millions. The financial crisis of 1873 is not the only cause of what followed in the twentieth century. But the line from the one to the other is a great deal shorter than most of us were taught.

Nobody figured it out at the time

My favorite passage in the book concerns the official inquiries. A U.S. congressional committee heard from more than seventy-five witnesses — a plumber, a physician, a piano builder, three cigar makers, and one man who described himself simply as a capitalist. An observer later extracted 180 distinct causes of the depression from the testimony. Five temperance representatives blamed intoxicating drink.

Every national commission found a convenient local villain: locusts in the American Midwest, foreign competition in Britain, the price of beet sugar in Germany, phylloxera in the French vineyards. Not one identified the demonetization of silver.

The closest anyone came to a consensus was that the depression traced back to excessive speculation during the boom, fueled by poor bank lending. The financial journalist Horace White put it best: "A financial crisis consists of an undue accumulation of debts based upon exaggerated and fanciful ideas of the value of property. Perhaps we have a right to expect that bankers will be wiser than others in discerning the approach of these catastrophes … but, in fact, they are not wiser than others, they never have been. They live in the same atmosphere as other people, and when it becomes surcharged with the oxygen of high prices, large profits, active speculation, and a delusive prosperity, they become exhilarated with the rest, and make large loans."

It ended, eventually, without anyone solving it. By the mid-1890s world gold production had jumped 50 percent, South Africa had overtaken the United States as the largest producer, and prices began rising again. The great deflation was broken by geology, not by policy.

Three things I'm taking away

Capital structure is the variable that matters. The 1870s boom was financed with debt, and the bust took down the bond market. The boom of the 1880s was comparably large — 75,000 miles of new U.S. track, more than doubling the network at a cost of $5 billion, or about 5 percent of annual GDP — but the railroads relied more on equity and less on debt, and it did not produce the same wreckage. When I look at today's infrastructure buildout, the question I care about is not the headline capex number that matches Ahamed's $600 billion. It is how much of it is equity-funded and how much is not.

The crash is rarely the catastrophe. A 6 percent decline in industrial production is a recession. Twenty years of deflation is a different thing entirely, and it came from a monetary decision made hastily by several governments at once, each of which thought it was acting reasonably. Errors of that kind do not announce themselves; the commissions that convened afterward never even nominated it as a suspect.

Watch the distribution, not just the aggregate. Growth was roughly normal for two decades. Purchasing power for urban wage earners improved. And the political system nearly came apart anyway, because a sustained transfer from debtors to creditors is felt by the people it happens to long before it appears in a national statistic. The aggregate data would have told you nothing was wrong.

1873 was, as Ahamed notes, as vivid to the generation that came of age in the early twentieth century as 1929 is to us. That it has been almost entirely forgotten is its own kind of warning.
